# Dashboard & Information-Display Design Canon, applied to a "signals / days worth looking at" panel

Scope: concrete, citable rules from Stephen Few, Edward Tufte, Nielsen Norman Group (NN/g), Shneiderman, Cowan/Miller/Hick, IBCS, and management-reporting practice, as they bear on one exceptions panel on an internal analytics dashboard (1–3 analysts, viewed a few times a week, daily aggregated data).

Where I could only reach an author's wording through a secondary source (e.g., Juice Analytics quoting Few's book, a reader's summary of Tufte), that is flagged inline.

## Key Question 1 — Stephen Few: dashboard definition, 13 pitfalls, exception highlighting, bullet graphs, single screen, "eyes drawn to what matters", color for alerts, gauges/clutter

### Takeaway
Few's canon for a monitoring display is: one screen, high-level summaries plus exceptions, neutral/subdued everything except the few items that currently need attention, no more than two alert levels (encoded by intensity of one hue, not red/amber/green), and "be conservative when determining the conditions that trigger alarms." Gauges, decoration and gratuitous color are rejected because they consume space and attention without adding information.

### Cited Findings

**Definition and purpose**
- Few's definition (first published March 2004, Intelligent Enterprise): "A dashboard is a visual display of the most important information needed to achieve one or more objectives; consolidated and arranged on a single screen so the information can be monitored at a glance." — [Few, "Dashboard Confusion", 2004 (PDF)](https://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/ie/dashboard_confusion.pdf)
- "The means is a single-screen display; the purpose is to efficiently keep in touch with the information needed to do something." — [Few, "Dashboard Confusion", 2004](https://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/ie/dashboard_confusion.pdf)
- "High-level summaries. The information displayed in a dashboard should consist primarily of high-level summaries, including exceptions, to communicate at a glance. It quickly tells you what's happening, but not why it's happening ... Diagnosis requires further investigation and detail. A dashboard can serve as the starting point for this investigation, letting you drill down into further detail." — [Few, "Dashboard Confusion", 2004](https://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/ie/dashboard_confusion.pdf)
- "Concise, clear, and intuitive display mechanisms. Display mechanisms that clearly state their message without taking up much space are required so the entire collection of information will fit into the limited real estate of a single screen." — [Few, "Dashboard Confusion", 2004](https://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/ie/dashboard_confusion.pdf)
- 2007 clarification: "The greatest clarification that is needed today is a distinction between dashboards, which are used for monitoring what's going on, and displays that combine several charts on a screen for the purpose of analysis." — [Few, "Dashboard Confusion Revisited", 2007 (PDF)](https://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/visual_business_intelligence/dboard_confusion_revisited.pdf)
- Summaries-and-exceptions passage from *Information Dashboard Design* (quoted by Juice Analytics; secondary): "The best way to condense a broad spectrum of information to fit onto a dashboard is in the form of summaries and exceptions… given the purpose of a dashboard to help people monitor what's going on, much of the information it presents is necessary only when something unusual is happening; something that falls outside the realm of normality, into the realm of problems and opportunities. Why make someone wade through hundreds of values when only one or two require attention? We call these critical values exceptions." — [Juice Analytics, "A Dashboard Alerts Checklist", quoting Few](https://www.juiceanalytics.com/writing/dashboard-alerts-checklist)

**The visual-monitoring process a dashboard must support (Few's three steps)**
- "they must be designed to support the following process of visual monitoring: 1. See the big picture. 2. Focus in on the specific items of information that need attention. 3. Quickly drill into additional information that is needed to take action. Step #3 can be supported through convenient links to additional information, but steps #1 and #2 require visual design that allows viewers to scan a great deal of information quickly to get an overview, easily recognize those items that demand attention, and then get enough information about those items to assess the potential need for a response." — [Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design", 2005/2006 (ProClarity whitepaper PDF)](https://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/Whitepapers/Common_Pitfalls.pdf)
- "This single-screen display need not be comprehensive in and of itself, but it must provide the overview that is needed to know when action is required, and ideally should provide an easy gateway to any additional information that is needed to determine the precise action that is appropriate." — [Few, "Common Pitfalls"](https://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/Whitepapers/Common_Pitfalls.pdf)

**The 13 common pitfalls (exact headings)**
- #1 Exceeding the Boundaries of a Single Screen; #2 Supplying Inadequate Context for the Data; #3 Displaying Excessive Detail or Precision; #4 Expressing Measures Indirectly; #5 Choosing Inappropriate Media of Display; #6 Introducing Meaningless Variety; #7 Using Poorly Designed Display Media; #8 Encoding Quantitative Data Inaccurately; #9 Arranging the Data Poorly; #10 Ineffectively Highlighting What's Important; #11 Cluttering the Screen with Useless Decoration; #12 Misusing or Overusing Color; #13 Designing an Unappealing Visual Display. — [Few, "Common Pitfalls"](https://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/Whitepapers/Common_Pitfalls.pdf)
- Pitfall #1 rationale: "Something critical is sacrificed when the viewer must lose sight of some data in order to scroll down or over, or move from screen to screen to see the rest. Part of the problem is that we can only hold a few chunks of information at a time in short-term memory ... One of the great benefits of a dashboard is the simultaneity of vision, the ability to see everything you need at once." Also: "Splitting the big picture into a series of separate small pictures is a mistake when seeing the big picture is worthwhile." — [Few, "Common Pitfalls"](https://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/Whitepapers/Common_Pitfalls.pdf)
- Pitfall #3: "dashboards should never display information that is more detailed or precise than necessary ... With a dashboard, every unnecessary piece of information results in wasted time, which is intolerable when time is definitely of the essence." — [Few, "Common Pitfalls"](https://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/Whitepapers/Common_Pitfalls.pdf)
- Pitfall #4 (indirect measures): "A percentage more clearly focuses attention on the variance itself" (context: show variance as −10% rather than making the reader compute actual vs target). — [Few, "Common Pitfalls"](https://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/Whitepapers/Common_Pitfalls.pdf)
- Pitfall #9: "The most important data ought to be prominent. Data that requires immediate attention ought to stand out. Data that should be compared ought to be arranged and visually designed to encourage comparisons." And: the top-left is "the most prominent position"; using it for a logo is "a waste of prime real estate". — [Few, "Common Pitfalls"](https://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/Whitepapers/Common_Pitfalls.pdf)
- Pitfall #10 ("the eye should go to what matters"): "because everything in this dashboard is visually prominent and vying for your attention, nothing in particular grabs your attention. When this happens the dashboard has failed. You should be able to look at a dashboard and have your eyes immediately drawn to the information that is most important. When everything is visually prominent, nothing stands out. All of the data displayed on a dashboard ought to be important, but not all data are equally important. When you are monitoring the business, your eyes must be drawn to those items that most need your attention right now." — [Few, "Common Pitfalls"](https://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/Whitepapers/Common_Pitfalls.pdf)
- Pitfall #11: decoration "becomes an absurd distraction from the task at hand ... Graphics dedicated to this end are pure decoration, visual content that the viewer must process to get to the data." — [Few, "Common Pitfalls"](https://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/Whitepapers/Common_Pitfalls.pdf)
- Pitfall #12: "When any color appears as a contrast to the norm, our eyes pay attention and our brains attempt to assign meaning to that difference ... We merrily assume that we can use colors like red, yellow and green to assign important meanings to data, but in doing so we exclude the 10% of males and 1% of females who are color blind. A common problem is the use of too many colors, especially bright colors ... When overused, color loses its power to highlight what's most important ... It is best to keep colors subdued and neutral, except when you are using color to highlight something as especially important." — [Few, "Common Pitfalls"](https://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/Whitepapers/Common_Pitfalls.pdf)

**Alerts / exception highlighting (Few's most specific rules)**
- Listed design problems to avoid on a monitoring dashboard: "Too much complexity; Too many alert conditions; Alerts that cannot be differentiated; Overwhelming visuals; Distracting visuals; Inappropriate visual salience; Mismatch between information and its visual representation; Indirect expression of measures; Not enough context." — [Few, "Dashboard Design for Real-Time Situation Awareness" (PDF)](https://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/Whitepapers/Dashboard_Design.pdf)
- "Don't display more information on the dashboard than the amount that can be quickly scanned and understood. How much is too much depends a great deal on how you present it ... when properly designed, 10,000 values can sometimes appear in a single chart in a way that is easy to read and understand." Also: "the high level of information density that works quite well for dashboards that people view once a day and respond to without urgency would exceed the practical perceptual limits of real-time monitoring." — [Few, "Situation Awareness"](https://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/Whitepapers/Dashboard_Design.pdf)
- "appropriate boundaries for the level of complexity cannot be established arbitrarily or generally; where the lines are drawn depends entirely on the audience." — [Few, "Situation Awareness"](https://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/Whitepapers/Dashboard_Design.pdf)
- Too many alert conditions: "If alarms go off often but rarely require a response, people soon learn to ignore them ... Be conservative when determining the conditions that trigger alarms. Don't demand immediate attention to circumstances that can wait. Only use visual or auditory means to draw attention to information that urgently requires a response. If things are fine, you don't need to draw attention to the fact. For example, I've seen many dashboards that use color coding to score every single item on the dashboard as good, satisfactory, or bad. When this is done, the abundance of color makes the few items that really need attention much harder to pick out from the rest. The counter-effect of crying wolf too often is so fundamentally true and recognized, that it has found its way into folklore." — [Few, "Situation Awareness"](https://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/Whitepapers/Dashboard_Design.pdf)
- "Alerts need not be restricted to conditions that are bad; they can be used to highlight especially good conditions also. What determines that attention is required is not whether something is bad or good, but whether or not it requires a response." — [Few, "Situation Awareness"](https://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/Whitepapers/Dashboard_Design.pdf)
- Alerts that cannot be differentiated: "No colors are used more often in dashboards to assign qualitative assessments to data than green, yellow, and red. Despite how prolific they are, they serve the purpose poorly, because 10% of males and 1% of females cannot distinguish between green and red ... The only reason that traffic lights work for people with this perceptual deficiency is because their meanings are redundantly encoded by their position." — [Few, "Situation Awareness"](https://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/Whitepapers/Dashboard_Design.pdf)
- "If possible, stick with a single visual means to draw attention to particular items on the dashboard, such as a simple icon like the red circle ... Something as simple as a red dot can work effectively as an alert if colors are used sparingly and red is only used for this purpose." — [Few, "Situation Awareness"](https://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/Whitepapers/Dashboard_Design.pdf)
- Number of severity levels: "On a real-time dashboard for situation awareness, use no more than two types of alerts at most. Rather than using different hues to differentiate them, if you use a single hue but vary its intensity from light to dark or pale to bright, even someone who is color blind will have no problem distinguishing them." — [Few, "Situation Awareness"](https://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/Whitepapers/Dashboard_Design.pdf)
- "The best use of color on a dashboard is for making particular items stand out from the rest ... assuming that the color red hasn't been used elsewhere and that the general tone of the dashboard is neutral in color, those items can be spotted without even thinking." — [Few, "Situation Awareness"](https://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/Whitepapers/Dashboard_Design.pdf)
- Dashboard as external memory: "By placing all of the information that you need to monitor (at least at a high level) on a single screen, simultaneously available to our eyes, we work around the limitations of short term memory by reducing the need to rely on it ... the dashboard serves as an external form of memory." — [Few, "Situation Awareness"](https://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/Whitepapers/Dashboard_Design.pdf)
- Judging criteria in Few's 2006 dashboard competition: "A single-screen display ... Display media ... which communicate performance information directly, clearly, accurately, rapidly, and with enough context to be meaningful; Visual emphasis on the information that most requires the viewer's attention; An arrangement of the information that is logical and supports the way ... executives would likely want to view it." Praise for the winner: "Color was used effectively to highlight what needs the most attention. The only time a non-gray color appears—red in this case—is when attention is required." Also, on ranked lists: executives "simply need an ordered list to alert them to these routes for possible action." — [Few, "Dashboard Design for Rich and Rapid Monitoring", 2006 (PDF)](https://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/visual_business_intelligence/dd_for_rapid_monitoring.pdf)

**Gauges and the dashboard metaphor**
- "Most meters, gauges and traffic lights that exist in virtually every dashboard product can be understood with little instruction. That's the advantage—the only advantage—of the dashboard metaphor. The joy of being greeted by those cute display widgets ... wears off after a few days, and what you're left with is a dashboard filled with gadgets that are just plain annoying and a poor use of valuable screen space." — [Few, "Dashboard Design: Taking a Metaphor Too Far", DM Review, March 2005 (PDF)](https://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/dmreview/dashboard_design.pdf)
- "Wouldn't it be helpful to also see 7,822 units in context, such as compared to a target or in relation to history, and to know not just that this value is good, but how good it is?" and "The purpose of a dashboard is to communicate a rich and often dense assortment of information in an instant. There is no place for useless content through which our eyes and brains must sort." — [Few, "Taking a Metaphor Too Far", 2005](https://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/dmreview/dashboard_design.pdf)
- Few cites Clark & Lyons (*Graphics for Learning*, 2004): "simple line drawings are a more effective form of communication than photographs and other detailed illustrations for most communication tasks because they show only what's needed." — [Few, "Taking a Metaphor Too Far", 2005](https://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/dmreview/dashboard_design.pdf)

**Bullet graph (Few's replacement for gauges; a model for graded severity)**
- "The bullet graph was developed to replace the meters and gauges that are often used on dashboards. Its linear and no-frills design provides [a rich display of data in a small] space, which is essential on a dashboard." Components: text label; quantitative scale; featured measure; one or two comparative measures (optional); qualitative ranges (optional). — [Few, "Bullet Graph Design Specification", rev. Oct 10, 2013 (PDF)](https://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/misc/Bullet_Graph_Design_Spec.pdf)
- Number of qualitative states: "The number of qualitative ranges should be limited to a maximum of five and ideally to three. More than five qualitative states would require a level of perceptual reasoning that is not efficient enough for a dashboard." — [Few, Bullet Graph Spec, 2013](https://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/misc/Bullet_Graph_Design_Spec.pdf)
- Encoding those states: "Rather than using distinct hues, which might not be distinguishable by those who are colorblind, encode these ranges as distinct intensities from dark to light of a single hue. Use the darker color intensities for the poor states and the lighter color intensities for the favorable states." Suggested grays — Two: 35%/10% black; Three: 40%/25%/10%; Four: 50/35/20/10; Five: 50/35/20/10/3. — [Few, Bullet Graph Spec, 2013](https://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/misc/Bullet_Graph_Design_Spec.pdf)
- "These ranges not only communicate the qualitative state of the featured measure but also the degree to which it resides within that state." — [Few, Bullet Graph Spec, 2013](https://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/misc/Bullet_Graph_Design_Spec.pdf)

**Few's nine color rules (chart-level, but directly applicable to severity encoding)**
- Rule #3 "Use color only when needed to serve a particular communication goal." Rule #4 "Use different colors only when they correspond to differences of meaning in the data." Rule #5 "Use soft, natural colors to display most information and bright and/or dark colors to highlight information that requires greater attention." Rule #7 "Non-data components of tables and graphs should be displayed just visibly enough to perform their role, but no more so, for excessive salience could cause them to distract attention from the data." Rule #8 "To guarantee that most people who are colorblind can distinguish groups of data that are color coded, avoid using a combination of red and green in the same display." Rule #9 "Avoid using visual effects in graphs." — [Few, "Practical Rules for Using Color in Charts", 2008 (PDF)](https://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/visual_business_intelligence/rules_for_using_color.pdf)
- "Consciously or not, when people look at a data display and see visual differences, they try to determine the meaning to those differences. Suggesting meanings, which aren't there, wastes people's time." — [Few, "Practical Rules for Using Color", 2008](https://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/visual_business_intelligence/rules_for_using_color.pdf)
- "A good substitute in a heatmap for distinguishing positive and negative values is blue and red, rather than green and red." — [Few, "Practical Rules for Using Color", 2008](https://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/visual_business_intelligence/rules_for_using_color.pdf)

### Inferences
- Mapped to a "signals" panel: the panel should show only items that "require a response" (not all days scored), use at most two severity tiers, encode them by intensity of one hue (or a single marker) rather than red/amber/green, keep every non-signal day visually neutral, and give each signal enough context (what deviated, from what baseline, by how much as a percentage) to decide "ignore for now or act" without leaving the panel.
- Few's "no more than two types of alerts" is stated for real-time dashboards; his bullet-graph spec allows up to five (ideally three) qualitative states for a graded measure. For a weekly-reviewed daily-data panel, three graded levels is defensible; five or more is explicitly outside his guidance.
- Few's own caveat ("where the lines are drawn depends entirely on the audience") supports a sensible default calibrated to the 1–3 expert analysts, rather than a general-purpose threshold.

### Gaps
- *Now You See It* (2009) and the 2nd edition of *Information Dashboard Design* (2013) are not online; the "summaries and exceptions" passage is available only via Juice Analytics' quotation. I found no primary-source text for the 2nd-edition wording on alerts or sparklines.
- Few's "Best Practices for Scaling Sparklines in Dashboards" PDF exists at perceptualedge.com but was not fetched (tool-call budget).

## Key Question 2 — Edward Tufte: data-ink ratio, chartjunk, small multiples, sparklines, layering and separation, smallest effective difference

### Takeaway
Tufte's principles that transfer to a signals list are: maximize data-ink and erase non-data ink; use the smallest visual difference that still reads; manage layering so secondary elements (gridlines, labels, non-flagged rows) recede; and use sparklines with a gray "normal range" band and one accent color for the point that matters.

### Cited Findings
- Sparkline definition (primary): "a small intense, simple, word-sized graphic with typographic resolution"; also "data-intense, design-simple, word-sized graphics." Guiding principle: "max[data], min[design]." — [Tufte, "Sparkline theory and practice", edwardtufte.com, 2004](https://www.edwardtufte.com/notebook/sparkline-theory-and-practice-edward-tufte/)
- On sparklines in lists: Tufte uses a colored accent (red) linking the rightmost data point to its number, and gray bands to show the normal range so deviations from expected limits are visible at once; frames, boxes and background colors "contribute little value" and the physical alignment of numbers, words and graphics forms an implicit grid. (Paraphrase of the fetched page.) — [Tufte, "Sparkline theory and practice"](https://www.edwardtufte.com/notebook/sparkline-theory-and-practice-edward-tufte/)
- Sparklines are "approximately right rather than exactly wrong"; a financial table with sparklines can carry ~14,000 readable numbers in the space of 24 precise figures. — [Tufte, "Sparkline theory and practice"](https://www.edwardtufte.com/notebook/sparkline-theory-and-practice-edward-tufte/)
- Sparklines "have a data-ink or data-pixel ratio = 1.0, consisting entirely of data, with no non-data at all"; they were driven by three principles: maximize data density, minimize or zero-out non-data, and the shrink principle. — search summary of Tufte's page; [Wikipedia, "Sparkline"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sparkline) / [edwardtufte.com](https://www.edwardtufte.com/notebook/sparkline-theory-and-practice-edward-tufte/)
- Data-ink ratio (secondary summary of *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*): "The proportion of a graphic's ink devoted to the non-redundant display of data-information"; rule: maximize it, erase non-data ink and redundant data-ink. — [Buteau, "Lessons from Edward Tufte" (secondary)](https://www.antoinebuteau.com/lessons-from-edward-tufte.md)
- Chartjunk (secondary): visual elements that distract rather than clarify — heavy grids, fake 3D, decoration; Tufte distinguishes administrative/tool-generated clutter from designer embellishment. — [Buteau (secondary)](https://www.antoinebuteau.com/lessons-from-edward-tufte.md)
- Small multiples (secondary, quoting *Envisioning Information*): "Illustrations of postage-stamp size, indexed by category or a label, sequenced over time like the frames of a movie." — [Buteau (secondary)](https://www.antoinebuteau.com/lessons-from-edward-tufte.md)
- Layering and separation / "1+1=3" (secondary): two visual elements placed close together create an unintended third effect; manage it via subtle layering (light gray grids, not heavy lines). — [Buteau (secondary)](https://www.antoinebuteau.com/lessons-from-edward-tufte.md); also [search summary of UMich Tufte notes](https://websites.umich.edu/~mmmc/516/notes/TuftePrinciples.pdf)
- Smallest effective difference (secondary, from *Envisioning Information*): "Make all visual distinctions as subtle as possible, but still clear and effective." — [UMich Tufte principles notes (secondary)](https://websites.umich.edu/~mmmc/516/notes/TuftePrinciples.pdf); same idea in [Buteau](https://www.antoinebuteau.com/lessons-from-edward-tufte.md) ("use the subtlest distinction possible ... rather than loud contrasts").
- Emphasis tactics attributed to Tufte (secondary): direct labeling beside data instead of legends; "ghosting" secondary elements while preserving context; gray as "the most important color in information design." — [Buteau (secondary)](https://www.antoinebuteau.com/lessons-from-edward-tufte.md)

### Inferences
- For a signals list: each row can carry a sparkline of the day's metric over the baseline window with a gray normal-range band and one accent mark on the flagged day — this is Tufte's own sparkline pattern and is a compact "deviation from normal" display.
- "Smallest effective difference" argues for severity tiers distinguished by modest intensity steps rather than saturated hues, which converges with Few's single-hue-intensity rule.
- IBCS explicitly discourages sparklines (see KQ6), so the two canons conflict on this device; Few and Tufte both endorse sparklines.

### Gaps
- Exact book wording for data-ink ratio, chartjunk, layering/separation and smallest effective difference could only be confirmed via secondary summaries; the books are not online. Page numbers unavailable.

## Key Question 3 — Progressive disclosure (NN/g) and Shneiderman's Visual Information-Seeking Mantra

### Takeaway
Show a few of the most important options/items first and reveal the rest on request (Nielsen); rarely go beyond two disclosure levels; and structure exploration as "Overview first, zoom and filter, then details-on-demand" (Shneiderman 1996).

### Cited Findings
- Progressive disclosure definition: "Initially, show users only a few of the most important options"; "Offer a larger set of specialized options upon request." — [Nielsen, "Progressive Disclosure", NN/g, Dec 3 2006](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/progressive-disclosure/)
- Benefits: improves learnability, efficiency and error rate; novices focus on essential features, advanced users avoid scanning rarely used options. Two design requirements: get the split between initial and secondary features right, and make the mechanics of progressing obvious. Designs "rarely succeed beyond 2 disclosure levels." Evidence claim: "people understand a system better when you help them prioritize features." — [Nielsen, "Progressive Disclosure", 2006](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/progressive-disclosure/)
- Progressive vs staged disclosure: progressive shows core features with secondary ones reachable hierarchically (users may never open them); staged disclosure is a linear sequence of steps. — [Nielsen, "Progressive Disclosure", 2006](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/progressive-disclosure/)
- Shneiderman's mantra, as stated: "Overview first, zoom and filter, then details-on-demand." Tasks listed: overview, zoom, filter, details-on-demand, relate, history, extract. Citation: Ben Shneiderman, "The Eyes Have It: A Task by Data Type Taxonomy for Information Visualizations," Proc. IEEE Symposium on Visual Languages, pp. 336–343, 1996. — [InfoVis Wiki, "Visual Information-Seeking Mantra"](https://infovis-wiki.net/wiki/Visual_Information-Seeking_Mantra); original PDF at [UMD](https://www.cs.umd.edu/~ben/papers/Shneiderman1996eyes.pdf)
- Few's three-step monitoring process (see KQ1) is the dashboard-specific analogue: big picture → items that need attention → drill into detail to act. — [Few, "Common Pitfalls"](https://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/Whitepapers/Common_Pitfalls.pdf)
- NN/g on list entries (what belongs in the row vs the detail page): "Too much will overload users and prevent them from seeing enough choices in one view, too little will make them pogo stick." Recommends a consistent structure across entries, high-priority information top-left, bold/larger type for key attributes, and treating each entry as a "small webpage" with a visual path through attributes in order of importance. — [Flaherty, "The Anatomy of a List Entry", NN/g, Apr 10 2016](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/list-entries/)

### Inferences
- A signals panel is a natural two-level disclosure: level 1 = the short ranked list (overview), level 2 = per-signal detail (which metric, baseline, magnitude, chart) opened on demand. Nielsen's "rarely beyond two levels" argues against nesting a third layer (e.g., per-detector tuning) inside the panel.
- Shneiderman's "filter" step corresponds to a sensitivity/threshold control; the mantra places it after overview, which supports a default overview that works without touching any control.
- Applied to alert/incident lists this is the common pattern "summary row → expand → full record"; I found no primary NN/g article specifically on alert/incident lists (see Gaps).

### Gaps
- Shneiderman's per-task descriptions (his own wording for overview/zoom/filter/details) could not be extracted: the UMD PDF is an image scan and the InfoVis Wiki page only lists task names.
- No NN/g or Few article was found that applies the mantra explicitly to "alert/incident lists"; the application above is inference.

## Key Question 4 — Nielsen's 10 usability heuristics that bear on an exceptions panel

### Takeaway
Four heuristics apply directly: keep users informed of status (#1), make options visible so nothing must be remembered (#6), remove every unit of information that is irrelevant or rarely needed because it competes with what matters (#8), and state problems in plain language with a constructive next step (#9).

### Cited Findings
- #1 Visibility of system status: "The design should always keep users informed about what is going on, through appropriate feedback within a reasonable amount of time." — [Nielsen, "10 Usability Heuristics for User Interface Design", NN/g, 1994, updated Jan 30 2024](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ten-usability-heuristics/)
- #3 User control and freedom: "Users often perform actions by mistake. They need a clearly marked 'emergency exit' to leave the unwanted action without having to go through an extended process." — [NN/g heuristics](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ten-usability-heuristics/)
- #6 Recognition rather than recall: "Minimize the user's memory load by making elements, actions, and options visible. The user should not have to remember information from one part of the interface to another." — [NN/g heuristics](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ten-usability-heuristics/)
- #8 Aesthetic and minimalist design: "Interfaces should not contain information that is irrelevant or rarely needed. Every extra unit of information in an interface competes with the relevant units of information and diminishes their relative visibility." — [NN/g heuristics](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ten-usability-heuristics/)
- #9 Help users recognize, diagnose, and recover from errors: "Error messages should be expressed in plain language (no error codes), precisely indicate the problem, and constructively suggest a solution." — [NN/g heuristics](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ten-usability-heuristics/)
- Related NN/g taxonomy: an *indicator* is "a way of making a page element ... stand out to inform the user that there is something special" about it — contextual, conditional, passive; *notifications* are "informational messages that alert the user of general occurrences within a system"; intrusiveness should match whether action is required ("action-required notifications should be intrusive", passive ones "less intrusive"). — [Flaherty, "Indicators, Validations, and Notifications", NN/g, Jan 17 2024](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/indicators-validations-notifications/)

### Inferences
- #1 implies the panel should state its own status plainly: data as-of date, baseline window used, and current sensitivity setting — so an empty list reads as "nothing flagged" rather than "broken".
- #8 is the heuristic-level justification for hiding unflagged days and secondary detectors by default.
- #9 translates to signal wording: name the metric, the direction and size of deviation, the comparison baseline, and (where possible) a suggested next look.

### Gaps
- None material; wording is primary.

## Key Question 5 — Cognitive load / working memory: Miller 7±2, Cowan 4±1, Hick's law, NN/g on too many controls

### Takeaway
Modern evidence puts working-memory capacity at about four chunks (Cowan), not seven; decision time grows with the log of the number of options (Hick–Hyman); NN/g's practical rules are to keep needed information visible (external memory), avoid clutter, prefer good defaults, and keep option counts "at a reasonable level" — no authority gives a fixed numeric limit for list items or filters.

### Cited Findings
- Cowan (2001) abstract: Miller's number "was meant more as a rough estimate and a rhetorical device than as a real capacity limit"; the review concludes "the smaller capacity limit is real" with "a single, central capacity limit averaging about four chunks" (range three to five) under conditions where chunking and rehearsal are prevented. Citation: Cowan, N. (2001). "The magical number 4 in short-term memory: A reconsideration of mental storage capacity." *Behavioral and Brain Sciences*, 24(1), 87–114. — [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/behavioral-and-brain-sciences/article/magical-number-4-in-shortterm-memory-a-reconsideration-of-mental-storage-capacity/44023F1147D4A1D44BDC0AD226838496); author PDF: [memory.psych.missouri.edu](https://memory.psych.missouri.edu/assets/doc/articles/2001/cowan-bbs-2001.pdf)
- Nielsen still cites the classic figure: "short-term memory famously holds only about 7 chunks of information, and these fade from your brain in about 20 seconds"; practical rules: fast response, visualize history (visited links), show comparisons side by side rather than across pages, don't make users remember codes, contextual help; designers "often exceed average user capacity." — [Nielsen, "Short-Term Memory and Web Usability", NN/g, Dec 6 2009](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/short-term-memory-and-web-usability/)
- NN/g 2018 update: capacity "depends on education, IQ, and age"; design rule: "make sure that users can easily access all the information they need for a task, without having to commit it to working memory"; "External memory refers to any tool or UI feature that allows users to explicitly save and access information needed during a task." Programmers tend to have larger spans and underestimate task difficulty. — [Budiu, "Working Memory and External Memory", NN/g, Apr 29 2018](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/working-memory-external-memory/)
- Cognitive load definition: "the amount of mental resources that is required to operate the system"; extraneous load is processing that "doesn't actually help users understand the content"; recommendations: avoid visual clutter, build on existing mental models, offload tasks (e.g., pre-filled data, smart defaults). — [Whitenton, "Minimize Cognitive Load to Maximize Usability", NN/g, Dec 22 2013](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/minimize-cognitive-load/)
- Hick's law: "The time it takes to make a decision increases with the number and complexity of choices." Origin: Hick (1952) and Hyman (1953). Takeaways include "Minimize choices when response times matter" and "Highlight recommended options." — [Laws of UX, "Hick's Law"](https://lawsofux.com/hicks-law/)
- Hick–Hyman formula RT = a + b·log2(n); because the relation is logarithmic, "the largest gains come from reducing small sets." — [Designorate, "Hick's Law: Building Usable Navigations"](https://www.designorate.com/hicks-law-building-usable-navigations/)
- Choice overload: "An excess of choices can lead to fatigue and can make people feel dissatisfied with the experience, or even worse, abandon the process altogether." "Humans have limited capacities for processing information and often choose the path of least effort." Recommendation: keep "the quantity of options at a reasonable level." — [Loranger, "Simplicity Wins over Abundance of Choice", NN/g, Nov 22 2015](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/simplicity-vs-choice/)
- Filters/controls: "The extra power of faceted navigation also adds interaction cost by presenting users with more options to comprehend and manipulate. A simple filter can often be easier to understand and faster to use." — [Whitenton, "Filters vs. Facets", NN/g, Mar 16 2014](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/filters-vs-facets/)
- Filter ordering: put "the most general, high-level filter categories at the top of the list, and the more specific ones towards the bottom"; "Relevance to users" is "the ultimate prioritization criterion"; specialized categories lower in the list "to avoid overwhelming beginners while keeping them available for experts." No numeric filter limit is given. — [Moran, "Defining Helpful Filter Categories and Values", NN/g, Jul 15 2018](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/filter-categories-values/)
- Few's dashboard-specific statement of the same limit: "we can only hold a few chunks of information at a time in short-term memory" (Pitfall #1) and the dashboard as "an external form of memory." — [Few, "Common Pitfalls"](https://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/Whitepapers/Common_Pitfalls.pdf); [Few, "Situation Awareness"](https://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/Whitepapers/Dashboard_Design.pdf)

### Inferences
- Working-memory limits bear on how many *distinct things the analyst must hold in mind at once* (e.g., severity levels, detector types, control states), not on the raw number of rows in a scrollable list; Cowan's ~4 suggests ≤4 severity tiers/detector categories visible at once, and Few's bullet-graph spec (ideally 3, max 5 states) is consistent.
- Hick's law argues that each extra control in the panel header has a real but diminishing cost; the largest benefit is going from several controls to one.
- No authority supports a hard "N items maximum" for a list; the defensible rule is Few's "no more than can be quickly scanned and understood" plus progressive disclosure for the tail.

### Gaps
- Vendor/blog claims such as "70% give up within 2 minutes when shown 12 filters" (bidux vignette, Aufait UX) are unverified marketing numbers; I found no NN/g or peer-reviewed source with a numeric limit on dashboard filters/controls.
- Hick (1952) / Hyman (1953) primary papers were not fetched; the law's statement is from secondary UX references.

## Key Question 6 — Exception reporting in management reporting / BI: management by exception, traffic-light pitfalls, IBCS notation, deviation-from-normal displays

### Takeaway
BI practice converges on: show exceptions, not everything; traffic lights are widely criticized (binary meaning, color-blindness, threshold gaming, overuse → overload); IBCS standardizes variance highlighting (green = good / red = bad relative to a target, consistent scenario notation, "avoid clutter", "increase information density", highlight with unified indicators); and Few/Tufte favor showing a value against its normal range (bullet graph ranges, sparkline gray band) rather than a bare status light.

### Cited Findings

**Management by exception / alerts checklists**
- Few, *Information Dashboard Design* (via Juice Analytics): "Why make someone wade through hundreds of values when only one or two require attention? We call these critical values exceptions." — [Juice Analytics quoting Few](https://www.juiceanalytics.com/writing/dashboard-alerts-checklist)
- Juice Analytics "Four C's" alerts checklist: Context ("Users need to understand how an alert is defined and how it fits into the larger picture"); Cogency ("avoid causing unnecessary alarm while delivering easy-to-understand information that can be acted upon"); Communication ("effectively capture attention and inform"); Control ("give users the ability to customize and manage alerts"). — [Gemignani, "A Dashboard Alerts Checklist", Juice Analytics](https://www.juiceanalytics.com/writing/dashboard-alerts-checklist)
- Few on ranked exception lists: executives "simply need an ordered list to alert them to these routes for possible action." — [Few, "Rich and Rapid Monitoring", 2006](https://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/visual_business_intelligence/dd_for_rapid_monitoring.pdf)

**Traffic-light / RAG pitfalls**
- Few: red/green excludes "10% of males and 1% of females"; traffic lights only work on the road because meaning is "redundantly encoded by their position"; scoring every item makes "the few items that really need attention much harder to pick out." — [Few, "Situation Awareness"](https://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/Whitepapers/Dashboard_Design.pdf)
- Bissantz (2005): "a traffic light provides two clear signals: red means stop, green means go," but "a company has countless ways in which it can react to red or green signals"; traffic-light functions multiply warnings through report hierarchies and green/yellow shades are hard to tell apart; he proposes parameter-free mechanisms (validated trend arrows, deviation corridors) that show "the way" rather than a stop signal. — [Bissantz, "Roadsigns, not red lights", Aug 19 2005](https://www.bissantz.de/en/bissantz-ponders/roadsigns-not-red-lights/)
- Marr (2021): pitfalls — overuse ("a long list of KPIs with traffic light colours next to them ... people quickly suffer from information overload"), unclear definitions ("open to interpretation"), gaming toward an "all-green screen" so "performance reporting becomes meaningless", colour blindness ("2.7 million colour blind people in the UK alone"). Recommendations: use sparingly on "specific, vital measures", define each colour, add symbols alongside colour, stick to three colours. — [Marr, "How to use traffic light colours and RAG ratings in dashboards", Jul 2 2021](https://bernardmarr.com/performance-reporting-how-to-use-traffic-light-colours-and-rag-ratings-in-dashboards/)
- NN/g dashboard article: "a red dot will easily stand out in a conglomerate of blue dots"; use colour "to reinforce information that is already communicated in a different way"; account for colour-blindness affecting up to 8% of males. — [Laubheimer, "Dashboards: Making Charts and Graphs Easier to Understand", NN/g, Jun 18 2017](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/dashboards-preattentive/)

**IBCS (International Business Communication Standards)**
- SUCCESS rule areas and one-line definitions: SAY "Convey a message"; UNIFY "Apply semantic notation"; CONDENSE "Increase information density"; CHECK "Ensure visual integrity"; EXPRESS "Choose proper visualization"; SIMPLIFY "Avoid clutter"; STRUCTURE "Organize content". — [IBCS overview page](https://www.ibcs.com/IBCS/)
- Highlighting: "Highlighting accelerates comprehension. Use unified indicators such as ellipses, trend arrows, and difference markers." — [IBCS overview page](https://www.ibcs.com/IBCS/)
- SIMPLIFY wording: "Avoid clutter: Reporting shall exclude redundant, distracting, or merely decorative components." — [IBCS Standards page (search summary)](https://ibcs.com/standards/)
- Scenario notation: actual (AC) solid dark fill, plan (PL) outlined/hollow, forecast (FC) hatched; variance markers "representing a positive impact on business issues (e.g. profit) are colored green; difference markers representing a negative impact ... are colored red." — [IBCS pages (search summary)](https://www.ibcs.com/IBCS/)
- "Top 4 – Variances: 'Green is good, and red is bad with respect to a given target.' Use red/green color exclusively for variance highlighting." "Bottom 3 – Cluttered Layout: 'Avoid clutter takes away the noise.' ... use color strategically for highlighting only." "Bottom 4 – Low Information Density: consolidate context onto single pages/screens." "Bottom 1 – Wrong Visualization: avoid pie charts, radar charts, and sparklines." — [IBCS, "Top and bottom 5 of IBCS"](https://www.ibcs.com/resource/top-and-bottom-5-of-international-business-communication-standards/)
- IBCS claims (its own study): SIMPLIFY and CONDENSE "primarily influence processing time", with "46% faster processing of information and 61% fewer errors"; UNIFY and CHECK "help readers to avoid errors." — [IBCS pages (search summary)](https://www.ibcs.com/IBCS/)

**Deviation-from-normal displays (Few / Tufte)**
- Few: pitfall #2 "Supplying Inadequate Context for the Data" — a measure alone "tells us nothing"; context such as a target or history is required; pitfall #4 — "A percentage more clearly focuses attention on the variance itself." — [Few, "Common Pitfalls"](https://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/Whitepapers/Common_Pitfalls.pdf)
- Few: bullet-graph qualitative ranges show "the degree to which it resides within that state," limited to 3–5 states, encoded by intensity of one hue. — [Few, Bullet Graph Spec, 2013](https://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/misc/Bullet_Graph_Design_Spec.pdf)
- Tufte: sparkline with gray normal-range band and red accent on the current value. — [Tufte, "Sparkline theory and practice"](https://www.edwardtufte.com/notebook/sparkline-theory-and-practice-edward-tufte/)
- Few on dashboards vs reality: only flag what "requires a response", whether good or bad. — [Few, "Situation Awareness"](https://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/Whitepapers/Dashboard_Design.pdf)

### Inferences
- The canon converges on a "graded deviation + sparse highlight" pattern: show the day's value against its normal band (baseline), express the deviation as a percentage or a difference marker, and reserve a single accent for days that pass the trigger — not a RAG light per day.
- IBCS's red/green variance convention and Few's anti-red/green rule conflict; a reconcilable position (used by both IBCS "add symbols" guidance via Marr, and Few's intensity rule) is to pair any colour with a non-colour cue (sign, arrow, position, text) and keep colour to one accent hue in the panel.
- IBCS's rejection of sparklines vs Few/Tufte's endorsement is a genuine disagreement; for a small internal panel, Few/Tufte are the more directly relevant authorities (IBCS targets formal management reports).

### Gaps
- IBCS rule-level wording with rule IDs (e.g., for highlighting, CONDENSE, SIMPLIFY sub-rules) could not be extracted: the v1.2 PDF exceeded the fetch size limit and the online standards pages render without text. Quotes above are from IBCS's overview and "top/bottom 5" pages and search summaries.
- Stacey Barr's "3 Problems with KPI Traffic Light Dashboards" and "5 Colour Mistakes" were blocked (HTTP 403); not cited.
- I found no primary-source definition of "management by exception" from a management-reporting standard; the concept is cited through Few's "exceptions" passage.

## Key Question 7 — User-adjustable parameters: expose a control vs set a sensible default

### Takeaway
NN/g's position is that users rarely change defaults or customize, so the default must be the good experience; offering options is "too easy" a way to dodge a design decision; expose a control only when it delivers a substantial benefit, and treat defaults as instruction and as the "commonly expected response."

### Cited Findings
- "It's too easy to resolve a design debate by simply offering all the possible options as preference settings and letting users decide the interface for themselves." Teams should instead produce "a single good, coherent user experience" and reserve customization for features with substantial benefits. "Customization happens when the user tells the computer what he or she prefers to see." — [Nielsen, "Customization of UIs and Products", NN/g, Aug 16 2009](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/customization-of-uis-and-products/)
- "Many users don't avail themselves of customization features. Users exhibit a strong bias toward simply getting things done on a website, rather than spending time fiddling with preference settings." — [Nielsen, "Customization of UIs and Products", 2009](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/customization-of-uis-and-products/)
- Users "rarely utilize fancy customization features"; defaults "serve as just-in-time instructions to help users understand how to complete a field" and communicate "the commonly expected response, as opposed to more atypical ones"; pre-populate with "the most common value"; a Cornell eye-tracking study showed the top-ranked position still drew ~34% of clicks when it was actually the second-best result, i.e., position/default bias is strong. — [Nielsen, "The Power of Defaults", NN/g, Sep 25 2005](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/the-power-of-defaults/)
- Offload tasks with "pre-filled data, or smart defaults wherever possible." — [Whitenton, "Minimize Cognitive Load", NN/g, 2013](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/minimize-cognitive-load/)
- Progressive disclosure: show "only a few of the most important options" initially; secondary options "upon request." — [Nielsen, "Progressive Disclosure", 2006](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/progressive-disclosure/)
- Few: "Be conservative when determining the conditions that trigger alarms"; the right complexity level "depends entirely on the audience." — [Few, "Situation Awareness"](https://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/Whitepapers/Dashboard_Design.pdf)
- Juice Analytics "Control": "Advanced alert systems should give users the ability to customize and manage alerts." — [Juice Analytics, alerts checklist](https://www.juiceanalytics.com/writing/dashboard-alerts-checklist)
- Bissantz argues for "parameter-free mechanisms" that highlight what needs attention "without parameterization." — [Bissantz, 2005](https://www.bissantz.de/en/bissantz-ponders/roadsigns-not-red-lights/)
- Hick's law: "Minimize choices when response times matter"; "Highlight recommended options." — [Laws of UX, "Hick's Law"](https://lawsofux.com/hicks-law/)

### Inferences
- For a panel used by 1–3 analysts a few times a week, the canon favours one conservative default (calibrated so that most days show no signal) and at most one visible control (a single sensitivity/threshold setting), with per-detector or per-day-kind parameters hidden behind a second disclosure level or kept in configuration.
- Because defaults also act as instruction, the panel should display the active setting (e.g., "sensitivity: normal; baseline: same weekday, last 8 weeks") so analysts can recognise, not recall, why a day is or is not flagged.
- Nielsen's "rarely beyond 2 disclosure levels" plus Hick's law argue against a settings tray with many sliders inside the panel.

### Gaps
- No NN/g article was found that studies dashboard threshold/sensitivity controls specifically; the guidance above is general customization/defaults guidance applied by inference.
