# Alert-fatigue design principles for a small-team, daily-batch "suspicious usage" dashboard

Scope note for the report writer: the target system is an internal dashboard that re-computes from daily aggregate data and is viewed by 1–3 analysts; there is no real-time paging. Most sources below assume on-call paging or 24/7 SOC/ICU operations. Each section's "Inferences" translates the paging-era rules into the daily-batch setting. Vendor sources (PagerDuty, Vectra, Ponemon/Damballa, iFactory, Kenexis, Emerson) are marked **[vendor]**; peer-reviewed or standards-body sources are marked **[primary]**.

Accessibility caveats: Rob Ewaschuk's original Google Doc returned HTTP 401 at both `/edit` and `/pub`; its content is captured here via the Prometheus docs (which cite it directly) and SRE-book chapter 6 (which Ewaschuk authored). Kokulu et al. (CCS 2019) full text was not reachable (404 on author mirror; only abstracts and the citation in Alahmadi et al.). EEMUA 191 / ISA-18.2 are paywalled standards; figures are taken from secondary summaries that state they copy the standards' tables. Honeycomb's site did not resolve from this environment; Charity Majors' personal blog was used instead.

## KQ1. What do the Google SRE book (ch. 6, 10, 11) and Rob Ewaschuk's "My Philosophy on Alerting" prescribe?

### Takeaway
Google's doctrine is: alert on user-visible *symptoms*, not causes; only page when the condition is urgent, actionable, novel, and requires human intelligence; route everything else to tickets or dashboards (not email); and cap human load explicitly (≤2 incidents per 12-hour shift, ~1:1 alert-to-incident ratio). SLO burn-rate alerting (SRE Workbook) is the quantitative embodiment: multi-window rules tuned for precision/recall, with the slowest-burning tier routed to a ticket rather than a page.

### Cited Findings

**Symptoms vs. causes (SRE book ch. 6, "Monitoring Distributed Systems", author Rob Ewaschuk, 2016) [primary]**
- "Your monitoring system should address two questions: what's broken, and why?" and "'What' versus 'why' is one of the most important distinctions in writing good monitoring with maximum signal and minimum noise." — [SRE Book ch.6](https://sre.google/sre-book/monitoring-distributed-systems/)
- Four golden signals: latency, traffic, errors, saturation. "If you measure all four golden signals and page a human when one signal is problematic (or, in the case of saturation, nearly problematic), your service will be at least decently covered." — [SRE Book ch.6](https://sre.google/sre-book/monitoring-distributed-systems/)

**The page criteria (ch. 6) [primary]**
- "Every time the pager goes off, I should be able to react with a sense of urgency." — [SRE Book ch.6](https://sre.google/sre-book/monitoring-distributed-systems/)
- "Every page should be actionable." — [SRE Book ch.6](https://sre.google/sre-book/monitoring-distributed-systems/)
- "Every page response should require intelligence. If a page merely merits a robotic response, it shouldn't be a page." — [SRE Book ch.6](https://sre.google/sre-book/monitoring-distributed-systems/)
- "Pages should be about a novel problem or an event that hasn't been seen before." — [SRE Book ch.6](https://sre.google/sre-book/monitoring-distributed-systems/)
- "Pages with rote, algorithmic responses should be a red flag." (Gmail scheduler example: a rote response signals the need for automation or a real fix, not a human page.) — [SRE Book ch.6](https://sre.google/sre-book/monitoring-distributed-systems/)
- Questions the chapter says to ask before adding any alert rule (paraphrased closely from the chapter's list): Does this rule detect an otherwise-undetected condition that is urgent, actionable, and actively/imminently user-visible? Will I ever be able to ignore this alert knowing it's benign — and when/why? Does this alert definitely indicate users are negatively affected (or are there cases such as drained traffic or test deployments to filter out)? Can I take action; is it urgent or could it wait until morning; could it be automated; is it a long-term fix or a short-term workaround? Are other people getting paged for the same issue (making one page redundant)? — [SRE Book ch.6](https://sre.google/sre-book/monitoring-distributed-systems/)

**Pages vs. tickets vs. logs/dashboards [primary]**
- Ch. 6 distinguishes three outputs (pages, tickets, email alerts) and says email alerts "tend to easily become overrun with noise" and are "rarely read or acted on". — [SRE Book ch.6](https://sre.google/sre-book/monitoring-distributed-systems/)
- Ch. 10 ("Practical Alerting"): "teams send their page-worthy alerts to their on-call rotation and their important but subcritical alerts to their ticket queues. All other alerts should be retained as informational data for status dashboards." — [SRE Book ch.10](https://sre.google/sre-book/practical-alerting/)
- Ch. 10 on flapping: "alerts can 'flap' (toggle their state quickly); therefore, the rules allow a minimum duration for which the alerting rule must be true before the alert is sent." (`for 2m` example). Alertmanager can "Inhibit certain alerts when others are active; Deduplicate alerts from multiple Borgmon that have the same labelsets". — [SRE Book ch.10](https://sre.google/sre-book/practical-alerting/)
- SRE Workbook, "Alerting on SLOs": "pages and tickets are the only valid ways to get a human to take action". — [SRE Workbook](https://sre.google/workbook/alerting-on-slos/)

**Noise/simplicity (ch. 6) [primary]**
- "To keep noise low and signal high, the elements of your monitoring system that direct to a pager need to be very simple and robust." and "The rules that catch real incidents most often should be as simple, predictable, and reliable as possible." — [SRE Book ch.6](https://sre.google/sre-book/monitoring-distributed-systems/)
- Bigtable case: the team was fatigued by SLO-based alerts driven by mean performance; they "temporarily dialed back the SLO target to the 75th percentile" and "disabled email alerts" to buy time to fix the underlying system — i.e., deliberately reduce alert sensitivity while a known structural problem is fixed. — [SRE Book ch.6](https://sre.google/sre-book/monitoring-distributed-systems/)

**Alert budgets / human load (ch. 11, "Being On-Call") [primary]**
- "we strive to invest at least 50% of SRE time into engineering" and "of the remainder, no more than 25% can be spent on-call, leaving up to another 25% on other types of operational, nonproject work." — [SRE Book ch.11](https://sre.google/sre-book/being-on-call/)
- "the maximum number of incidents per day is 2 per 12-hour on-call shift", derived from the finding that dealing with one incident (root cause, remediation, postmortem, bug fixes) "takes 6 hours". — [SRE Book ch.11](https://sre.google/sre-book/being-on-call/)
- "Noisy alerts that systematically generate more than one alert per incident should be tweaked to approach a 1:1 alert/incident ratio." — [SRE Book ch.11](https://sre.google/sre-book/being-on-call/)
- "Low-priority alerts that bother the on-call engineer every hour (or more frequently) disrupt productivity, and the fatigue such alerts induce can also cause serious alerts to be treated with less attention". — [SRE Book ch.11](https://sre.google/sre-book/being-on-call/)
- Cognition under stress: the chapter contrasts "Intuitive, automatic, and rapid action" with "Rational, focused, and deliberate cognitive functions" and notes "Stress hormones like cortisol and corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH) are known to cause behavioral consequences—including fear—that can impair cognitive functions". — [SRE Book ch.11](https://sre.google/sre-book/being-on-call/)

**SLO burn-rate alerting (SRE Workbook ch. 5) [primary]**
- Alerting quality is measured on four axes: Precision = "The proportion of events detected that were significant."; Recall = "The proportion of significant events detected."; Detection time = "How long it takes to send notifications in various conditions."; Reset time = "How long alerts fire after an issue is resolved." — [SRE Workbook](https://sre.google/workbook/alerting-on-slos/)
- Naive "error rate > SLO threshold" alerting has low precision and can produce "up to 144 alerts per day" while still meeting the SLO; a long window alone gives reset times of "36 hours". — [SRE Workbook](https://sre.google/workbook/alerting-on-slos/)
- "burn rate is how fast, relative to the SLO, the service consumes the error budget." Recommended multi-window multi-burn-rate table for a 99.9% SLO: Page at 14.4× burn over 1h (short window 5m, 2% of monthly budget); Page at 6× over 6h (short 30m, 5%); **Ticket** at 1× over 3 days (short 6h, 10%). "make the short window 1/12 the duration of the long window." — [SRE Workbook](https://sre.google/workbook/alerting-on-slos/)

**Rob Ewaschuk, "My Philosophy on Alerting" (2014) — via secondary sources**
- The Prometheus project's official alerting guide opens with "We recommend that you read My Philosophy on Alerting based on Rob Ewaschuk's observations at Google" and distils it as: "keep alerting simple, alert on symptoms, have good consoles to allow pinpointing causes, and avoid having pages where there is nothing to do." — [Prometheus alerting best practices](https://prometheus.io/docs/practices/alerting/)
- "Alerts should link to relevant consoles and make it easy to figure out which component is at fault." — [Prometheus](https://prometheus.io/docs/practices/alerting/)
- Batch/offline guidance (directly relevant to a daily-batch system): "For offline processing systems, the key metric is how long data takes to get through the system, so page if that gets high enough to cause user impact." and "For batch jobs it makes sense to page if the batch job has not succeeded recently enough, and this will cause user-visible problems. This should generally be at least enough time for 2 full runs of the batch job." — [Prometheus](https://prometheus.io/docs/practices/alerting/)
- Meta-monitoring: "It is important to have confidence that monitoring is working." — [Prometheus](https://prometheus.io/docs/practices/alerting/)
- Secondary summaries of Ewaschuk's doc state the four-adjective rule that pages should be "urgent, important, actionable, and real" and that one should page on user-facing symptoms rather than every possible cause; a cause-based alert is still justified "when catching the symptom would have been too late". — [search summary citing Prometheus/Ewaschuk](https://prometheus.io/docs/practices/alerting/); [Datadog "Monitoring 101: Alerting on what matters"](https://datadoghq.com/blog/monitoring-101-alerting) **[vendor]**

### Inferences (translation to a daily-batch, 1–3 analyst dashboard)
- The pages/tickets/dashboard triage maps onto: (a) a very small "look today" list (the page analogue — the SRE criteria of urgent + actionable + novel + requires judgment should gate entry); (b) a "review this week" queue (ticket analogue, cf. the Workbook's 3-day/1× burn-rate tier going to a ticket); (c) everything else as browsable context on the dashboard, never as a notification. The SRE book's explicit distrust of email alerts argues against a daily email that lists every flagged item.
- The SRE load budget (≤2 incidents per 12h shift, each ~6h of work) suggests sizing the "look today" tier against the analysts' available time rather than against detector output: if 1–3 analysts can each spend, say, 30–60 min/day, the daily tier should carry only a handful of items, each with a stated expected investigation time.
- "Approach a 1:1 alert/incident ratio" translates to: group all signals about the same user/day/workspace into one card rather than one line per detector; a single underlying event should produce a single item.
- The `for 2m` duration clause and the multi-window idea translate to "confirm over N consecutive days" or "short-window + long-window" rules on daily data (e.g., flag only if today's anomaly is also visible in a 7-day aggregate), trading detection time for precision — acceptable because nothing is real-time.
- The Bigtable case legitimises temporarily lowering sensitivity (as the repo already exposes a sensitivity control) while a known noisy source is fixed, provided the lowering is explicit and time-boxed.
- Ewaschuk's batch-job rule gives a concrete meta-alert for this system: the only thing that deserves an out-of-band notification is "the daily job did not succeed within 2 run periods" — i.e., monitor the pipeline, not just the data.

### Gaps
- Ewaschuk's original document could not be fetched (401); quotes attributed to it are only as relayed by Prometheus docs and SRE-book ch. 6. Exact wording of its "when you receive a page you should be able to…" checklist and its "tracking and accountability" section are not independently verified.
- The SRE book gives on-call load numbers but no explicit guidance on how many *non-paging* daily review items are tolerable; the inference above is derived, not sourced.

## KQ2. What do PagerDuty, Honeycomb/Charity Majors and similar practitioners say about alert fatigue and noise reduction?

### Takeaway
Practitioners converge on the same rules as Google with a few concrete additions: a review trigger (PagerDuty: >15 alerts/week → hold a debrief), a separate low-urgency path ("save it for morning"), deduplication/grouping into one incident, descriptive names plus a runbook link, weekly alert reviews, and (Majors) "delete all your paging alerts" and re-add only end-to-end user-pain alerts.

### Cited Findings
- PagerDuty's 7-step programme: (1) commit team time to clean up; (2) "Cut non-actionable alerts & adjust thresholds"; (3) "Save non-severe incidents for morning" via separate low-urgency workflows; (4) "Consolidate related alerts" with incident keys/alert bundling; (5) "Give alerts relevant names & descriptions" including "a link to existing documentation or runbooks that will help the team dig deeper"; (6) route to the right people; (7) "Keep it updated with regular reviews" (weekly). — [PagerDuty, "Let's talk about alert fatigue"](https://www.pagerduty.com/blog/uncategorized/lets-talk-about-alert-fatigue-2/) **[vendor]**
- PagerDuty's internal review threshold: "if an on-call team receives more than 15 alerts weekly, they conduct a debrief to review them." — [PagerDuty](https://www.pagerduty.com/blog/uncategorized/lets-talk-about-alert-fatigue-2/) **[vendor]**
- Actionability definition: raw metrics "like CPU and memory usage are NOT actionable because they don't give specific information about what's wrong". "While all alerts are important, some may not be urgent. These non-urgent issues shouldn't be waking you or your team up in the middle of the night." — [PagerDuty](https://www.pagerduty.com/blog/uncategorized/lets-talk-about-alert-fatigue-2/) **[vendor]**
- Charity Majors (2019): "delete all your paging alerts, then page only on e2e alerts that mean users are in pain." and "page only on high level end-to-end alerts, the ones which traverse the code paths that make you money and correspond to user pain". "Don't alert on the state of individual storage instances, or replication, or anything that isn't user-visible." — [charity.wtf, "Love (and Alerting) in the Time of Cholera (and Observability)"](https://charity.wtf/2019/09/20/love-and-alerting-in-the-time-of-cholera-and-observability/)
- Majors on urgency and novelty: "Every paging alert out of hours should be as serious as a heart attack" and "any time you get paged about a distributed system, it should be a genuinely new failure that requires your full creative attention". The post gives no numeric volume thresholds. — [charity.wtf](https://charity.wtf/2019/09/20/love-and-alerting-in-the-time-of-cholera-and-observability/)
- Majors' argument that good exploratory tooling (observability) removes the need for a "scattershot bunch of pagebombs": "Once you make the shift to observability...you no longer need to lean on that scattershot bunch of pagebombs to understand your systems." — [charity.wtf](https://charity.wtf/2019/09/20/love-and-alerting-in-the-time-of-cholera-and-observability/)
- Prometheus guidance (community, non-vendor): "Alerts should link to relevant consoles and make it easy to figure out which component is at fault." — [Prometheus](https://prometheus.io/docs/practices/alerting/)

### Inferences
- PagerDuty's ">15/week → debrief" is the only concrete practitioner volume trigger found; for a 1–3 person team reviewing daily, an analogous rule would be "if the top-tier list exceeds ~N items per week, hold a tuning review" with N chosen from analyst capacity (see KQ1 inference).
- Majors' "delete and re-add" is a useful launch policy for a new dashboard: start with zero notification-grade signals and promote detectors only after they have shown precision on historical data.
- "Name + description + runbook link" translates to every signal card carrying: what rule fired, which numbers, what "normal" was, and a one-paragraph "what to check next" — i.e., a per-detector playbook embedded in the UI.
- The emphasis on exploration tooling (Majors) supports making the dashboard's drill-down the primary product and the flagged list a secondary entry point.

### Gaps
- Honeycomb's own blog (e.g., "Actionable SLOs based on what matters most") could not be fetched (DNS failure); Honeycomb-specific statistics on alert reductions are not recorded.
- No practitioner source found that gives a validated *number of alerts per day* for a non-paging review queue.

## KQ3. What does academic SOC-analyst research find about why analysts ignore alerts, what they want in an alert, and how context/explainability affect triage?

### Takeaway
Peer-reviewed SOC studies find that most "false positives" are actually *benign triggers* (rule fired correctly on legitimate activity), that black-box alerts without a stated reason cause loss of trust and inaction, and that analysts validate alarms primarily through contextual knowledge (asset, business, working hours). Alahmadi et al. propose the REACT requirements — Reliable, Explainable, Analytical, Contextual, Transferable — and note that alarm validation "is a tedious task that can cause alarm burnout and eventually desensitization". Burnout research (Sundaramurthy) frames the problem as a management/human-capital vicious cycle rather than purely a tooling one.

### Cited Findings

**Alahmadi, Axon, Martinovic, "99% False Positives", USENIX Security 2022 [primary]** (survey n=20, interviews n=21 from seven SOCs, mostly UK)
- Headline quote (participant B3): "We know 99% of the alarms we generate are false positives, but we still have to look at them." — [Alahmadi et al. 2022](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/sec22-alahmadi.pdf)
- Survey: for "The monitoring tools I use frequently produce false positive results" the disagree:agree split was 2:14 (mode 4/5); for "The number of alerts generated by most IDS are overwhelming" it was 4:12. Analysts were neutral on whether IDS are inadequate at detecting attacks (7:6). — [Alahmadi et al. 2022, Table 3](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/sec22-alahmadi.pdf)
- Benign triggers vs false alarms: "False alarms are used to describe an alarm being generated without an actual security-related event (the boy who cried wolf). In contrast, benign triggers are real alarms, meaning they match an existing signature but the organization chooses to ignore them for a 'business-justified' purpose." Participant C5: "the condition is perfectly matched, and the filter as such works, but the circumstances are completely legitimate." C5 also: "The benign trigger probability is usually very high, from hands-on experience." — [Alahmadi et al. 2022 §6](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/sec22-alahmadi.pdf)
- "When the analyst reported a 99% FP rate, this is found to be mostly benign triggers and not necessarily a measurement of the performance of the technology itself." — [Alahmadi et al. 2022 §6](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/sec22-alahmadi.pdf)
- Why alerts get ignored — explainability: "the analyst receives an alert but does not know the reason for the detection"; descriptions "are incomprehensible or are short descriptions that do not explain why the alarm was produced"; "the lack of explainability causes alarm desensitization." C5: "if you don't tell me the reason you fire ... I can't have really high respect on that." B4: ambiguity "leads to uncertainty about the existence of an attack, resulting in no action from analysts." — [Alahmadi et al. 2022 §7.2](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/sec22-alahmadi.pdf)
- Reliability: poorly written signatures that fire constantly are useless — A1: "if it's just constantly firing, nobody's got the time to review all of them, so it has to be well written". Analysts "start their alarm-validation process by looking first at tools they 'trust' or consider 'reliable.'" — [Alahmadi et al. 2022 §7.1, §1](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/sec22-alahmadi.pdf)
- Context: analysts eliminate FPs with knowledge of "the network topology and devices, what these devices are used for, and their location and owner" and of the customer's business; E10 on working hours: "there is no traffic on a Friday"; an outbound-traffic alarm was explained by an acquisition. "Contextual knowledge about the network and systems can improve alert validation significantly ... analysts not only rely on technical knowledge ... but also knowledge about the business (e.g., work hours) and knowledge acquired from third parties". 35% of survey participants triage alarms according to newly announced vulnerabilities in security blogs. — [Alahmadi et al. 2022 §7.3, §5](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/sec22-alahmadi.pdf)
- Tuning loop: "Alarms are periodically reviewed by the analysts to tune the defined alarms further and eliminate FPs"; G19 reviews with customers whether alarms are within tolerance ("well we sort of want to know about this but we sort of don't"). "most of such tedious configurations fall on the analysts themselves." — [Alahmadi et al. 2022 §5](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/sec22-alahmadi.pdf)
- Burnout framing: "Alarm validation is a tedious task that can cause alarm burnout and eventually desensitization." "An excessive number of alarms, in any system, contributes to alarm desensitization, mistrust, and lack of human response". Analysts "are also under constant pressure to close tickets, as some SOCs evaluate their practitioners' performance accordingly" [citing Sundaramurthy et al.]. — [Alahmadi et al. 2022 Abstract, §1](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/sec22-alahmadi.pdf)
- Four perceived limitation classes: "(1) unreliable alarms ... (2) lack of customizability ... (3) black-box alarms and lack of explainability; and (4) lack of context on the networks and systems, process, or business". — [Alahmadi et al. 2022 §1](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/sec22-alahmadi.pdf)
- REACT requirements: Reliable (use "false alarms or benign triggers as an alternative metric to FPs"; feed analyst feedback back into prioritisation; avoid volatile/too-broad features; detect specific behaviours rather than "everything"); Explainable ("analysts actually should not trust the system completely. Based on system explanations, the analyst should know when to trust the system's predictions and when to apply their own judgment. Analysts' trust needs to be calibrated using properly designed prediction explanations."); Analytical (support the analyst's own hypothesis-driven reasoning rather than full automation); Contextual ("a SOC tool that monitors for abnormal network activity should consider the customer's work hours to eliminate benign triggers such as the absence of traffic on a Friday"); Transferable (configured per organisation, "should not be designed as one size fits all"). — [Alahmadi et al. 2022 §8](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/sec22-alahmadi.pdf)
- ML skepticism (B4): "humans aren't exactly going to be normal and do the same stuff every day, so I think that platforms like that themselves have a lot of false alarms." Only 2 of 20 surveyed (10%) used AI/ML monitoring tools. — [Alahmadi et al. 2022 §8](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/sec22-alahmadi.pdf)
- Target 2013 breach anecdote: the FireEye tool "detected the malware, generating an alarm"; when escalated "it was ignored, and no action was taken" — the paper's motivating example of an ignored true alert. — [Alahmadi et al. 2022 §1](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/sec22-alahmadi.pdf)
- Self-declared limitations: n=21, seven SOCs, mostly UK/Europe, non-random recruitment; "These limitations prevented us from extracting generalizable conclusions". — [Alahmadi et al. 2022 §9](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/sec22-alahmadi.pdf)

**Kokulu et al., "Matched and Mismatched SOCs", CCS 2019 [primary, abstract-level only]**
- 18 semi-structured interviews with SOC analysts and managers across sectors; the study "identified technical and non-technical issues" and "found inherent disagreements between SOC managers and their analysts that, if not addressed, could entail a risk to SOC efficiency and effectiveness." — [ASU Pure record](https://asu.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/matched-and-mismatched-socs-a-qualitative-study-on-security-opera)
- As cited by Alahmadi et al.: "Kokulu et al. have found that SOC security practitioners do not consider FPs in automatic malicious activity detection to be a significant issue in SOC operations. Such findings contradict academic beliefs on the prevalence of FPs in SOCs" — the apparent contradiction motivated the benign-trigger distinction. — [Alahmadi et al. 2022 §1](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/sec22-alahmadi.pdf)

**Sundaramurthy et al., "A Human Capital Model for Mitigating Security Analyst Burnout", SOUPS 2015 [primary, abstract-level only]**
- Method: "an anthropological study of a corporate SOC over a period of six months", Grounded Theory; model validated against fieldnotes from a university SOC. — [USENIX SOUPS 2015](https://usenix.org/conference/soups2015/proceedings/presentation/sundaramurthy)
- "burnout is a human capital management problem resulting from the cyclic interaction of a number of human, technical, and managerial factors." Existing remedies such as career progression "deal only with the symptoms". The model describes vicious cycles among analyst skills, empowerment, creativity and growth that cause analysts to lose morale and leave; the authors propose turning them into virtuous cycles. — [USENIX SOUPS 2015](https://usenix.org/conference/soups2015/proceedings/presentation/sundaramurthy)

**Hámornik & Krasznay (2017) [primary, abstract-level only]**
- "Prerequisites of Virtual Teamwork in Security Operations Centers" (interviews with SOC experts + literature): treating "the cybersecurity team as a socio-technical system and supporting to cope with challenges of virtual teams helps them to be more effective and enhances employee retention." — [REAL repository, PDF link available](https://real.mtak.hu/148177)
- Companion AHFE 2017 chapter "A Team-Level Perspective of Human Factors in Cyber Security: Security Operations Centers" frames SOC work as team cognition under time pressure — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/978-3-319-60585-2_21) (abstract not retrievable; access redirected to login).

**Industry surveys (volume statistics) [vendor-sponsored]**
- Ponemon/Damballa 2015 (630 US practitioners): "In a typical week an organization can receive an average of nearly 17,000 malware alerts. Of all alerts, 19 percent (3,218) are considered reliable but only 4 percent (705) are investigated." Time wasted on inaccurate alerts estimated at $1.27M/yr. — [Ponemon Institute (Damballa-sponsored)](https://www.ponemon.org/local/upload/file/Damballa%20Malware%20Containment%20FINAL%203.pdf)
- Vectra AI 2023 (2,000 SOC analysts): SOC teams "receive an average of 4,484 alerts per day"; 67% of alerts are ignored / unable to be dealt with; "83% report that alerts are false positives and not worth their time"; "78% said it takes 10 minutes or more to look into each alert"; teams spend "nearly three hours a day manually triaging alerts"; "97% of SOC analysts worry about missing a relevant security event because it's buried under a flood of alerts"; "41% believe alert overload is the norm because vendors are afraid of not flagging an event that could turn out to be important." — [Security Magazine summary of Vectra report](https://securitymagazine.com/articles/99674-90-of-soc-analysts-believe-current-threat-detection-tools-are-effective); [Vectra findings recap](https://infotrust.com.au/resource-library/vectra-2023-state-of-threat-detection-report-the-findings/blog)

### Inferences
- The single most transferable finding for a usage-anomaly dashboard is the benign-trigger concept: a usage spike that is "perfectly matched" by a rule but explained by a legitimate project is not a detector failure; the UI should therefore let analysts record a *benign explanation* (not just "false positive") and should suppress or down-weight repeats of the same explained pattern. This is also the empirical basis for avoiding "alert = guilt" framing (see KQ6).
- Per Alahmadi §7.2, every flagged item should state *why* it fired in plain terms (rule, observed value, baseline, comparison population) — black-box scores alone predictably produce inaction.
- Context analysts actually use (asset owner, business role, working hours, known projects, recent organisational changes) should be first-class fields on the card; the dashboard already compares against "same day kind" baselines, which is exactly the "no traffic on Friday" lesson.
- Calibrated trust (Alahmadi's Explainable requirement) implies showing per-detector historical precision (how often past flags were confirmed vs. explained-benign), so analysts know when to lean on the signal.
- The management angle (Sundaramurthy, Kokulu): do not measure analysts on "items closed"; measure on explained patterns and tuning improvements, or the volume incentive recreates the vicious cycle even at 1–3 people.
- Vendor-survey numbers (67% ignored, 10+ min/alert, 83% FP) are directionally consistent with the peer-reviewed qualitative findings but should be cited as marketing-grade evidence only.

### Gaps
- Kokulu et al. full text (specific quotes on false-positive attitudes, manager/analyst disagreements, tool metrics) not accessible; only the abstract and Alahmadi's citation are recorded.
- Sundaramurthy et al. full text could not be extracted (PDF text extraction failed); the specific vicious-cycle diagram and interventions are not quoted.
- Hámornik & Krasznay AHFE 2017 chapter abstract not retrieved (publisher login redirect).
- No peer-reviewed study found quantifying *analyst minutes per alert* or an optimal *alerts per analyst per day*; only vendor surveys give such numbers.

## KQ4. What do safety-critical alarm-management standards (ISA-18.2 / EEMUA 191) and clinical alarm-fatigue studies recommend?

### Takeaway
Process-industry standards define an alarm as something that *requires operator action* and set quantitative targets: ~6 alarms/operator/hour target (~150/day), >12/hour (300/day) considered unmanageable, floods defined as >10 alarms in 10 minutes, priority distribution ≈ 80% low / 15% medium / 5% high, and top-10 "bad actor" alarms should contribute <5% of load. Clinical evidence shows 85–99% of hospital alarms need no intervention and ~89% of ICU arrhythmia alarms are false, producing desensitisation with documented deaths; remedies focus on customising thresholds to the patient, tiering, and cross-disciplinary review.

### Cited Findings

**ISA-18.2 / EEMUA 191 (secondary summaries of the standards' tables)**
- Alarm definition per ISA-18.2 (three criteria): "(1) a defined operator action that differs from baseline response, (2) a definable consequence if unaddressed, and (3) sufficient time to respond." — [iFactory summary of ISA-18.2](https://ifactoryapp.com/blog/alarm-management-rationalization-chemical) **[vendor]**
- Alarm rate per operator: target "< 6 per hour (≈ 1 per 10 minutes)"; typical un-rationalised plants "30–150 per hour". — [iFactory](https://ifactoryapp.com/blog/alarm-management-rationalization-chemical) **[vendor]**
- Search-result summary of the standards (Emerson/ABB documents): "average alarm rate per operator target is less than 12 alarms per hour, with an action limit of greater than 18 alarms per hour"; and "ISA-18.2 and EEMUA 191 both publish that the priority distribution should be roughly 80% low / 15% medium / 5% high." (The Emerson PDF itself could not be fetched; figures as reported in search results.) — [Emerson "Alarm Management by Numbers" (unreachable)](https://d1-live.emerson.com/documents/automation/article-alarm-management-by-numbers-deltav-en-38292.pdf) **[vendor]**
- Note on discrepancy: sources differ on the hourly target (≈6/h "very likely acceptable" vs "<12/h" as the manageable ceiling); EEMUA 191's published table is commonly summarised as ~150/day acceptable and ~300/day maximum manageable, which corresponds to ~6/h and ~12/h respectively — the two figures are two rows of the same table, not a contradiction, but this could not be verified against the primary text.
- Overload rationale: "an operator can be considered 'overloaded' if they are receiving an alarm more than once every 5-10 minutes", because 5–10 minutes is the typical time to respond to one alarm; "EVERY alarm needs to be responded to". — [Kenexis](https://www.kenexis.com/?p=1327) **[vendor]**
- Alarm flood: "More than 10 alarms in 10 minutes per operator"; peak-upset target "< 10 alarms in 10 minutes" vs typical "100–400". — [iFactory](https://ifactoryapp.com/blog/alarm-management-rationalization-chemical) **[vendor]**
- Standing alarms target "< 5 per operator" (typical 20–80); stale alarms (>24h) target 0 (typical 5–20); chattering alarms target 0 (typical 10–30). — [iFactory](https://ifactoryapp.com/blog/alarm-management-rationalization-chemical) **[vendor]**
- Priority distribution H/M/L target "5% / 15% / 80%"; typical un-rationalised sites "28% / 41% / 31%". — [iFactory](https://ifactoryapp.com/blog/alarm-management-rationalization-chemical) **[vendor]**
- Bad actors: top-10 alarm tags should contribute "< 5% of total alarm load"; typical "40–60%". — [iFactory](https://ifactoryapp.com/blog/alarm-management-rationalization-chemical) **[vendor]**
- ISA-18.2 lifecycle (11 stages): Philosophy, Identification, Rationalization, Detailed Design, Implementation, Operation, Maintenance, Monitoring & Assessment, Management of Change, Audit, Continuous Improvement. — [iFactory](https://ifactoryapp.com/blog/alarm-management-rationalization-chemical) **[vendor]**

**Clinical alarm fatigue [primary]**
- Joint Commission Sentinel Event Alert 50 (2013): "The number of alarm signals per patient per day can reach several hundred depending on the unit within the hospital, translating to thousands of alarm signals on every unit and tens of thousands of alarm signals throughout the hospital every day. It is estimated that between 85 and 99 percent of alarm signals do not require clinical intervention, such as when alarm conditions are set too tight; default settings are not adjusted for the individual patient or for the patient population; ECG electrodes have dried out; or sensors are mispositioned." — [Joint Commission SEA 50](https://digitalassets.jointcommission.org/api/public/content/f65e5c9df2b94000a99445e0a7877007)
- Consequence: "clinicians become desensitized or immune to the sounds, and are overwhelmed by information – in short, they suffer from 'alarm fatigue.' In response to this constant barrage of noise, clinicians may turn down the volume of the alarm, turn it off, or adjust the alarm settings outside the limits that are safe and appropriate for the patient – all of which can have serious, often fatal, consequences." — [Joint Commission SEA 50](https://digitalassets.jointcommission.org/api/public/content/f65e5c9df2b94000a99445e0a7877007)
- Harm data: 98 alarm-related sentinel events Jan 2009–Jun 2012, "80 resulted in death, 13 in permanent loss of function, and five in unexpected additional care or extended stay"; FDA MAUDE: "566 alarm-related patient deaths were reported between January 2005 and June 2010", considered an underestimate. "Alarm fatigue – the most common contributing factor"; also "Alarm settings that are not customized to the individual patient or patient population". — [Joint Commission SEA 50](https://digitalassets.jointcommission.org/api/public/content/f65e5c9df2b94000a99445e0a7877007)
- Recommended actions include: "Re-establish priorities for the adoption of alarm technology; the priority-setting process should drive technology adoption rather than allowing technology to drive the process" and "Establish a cross-disciplinary team that includes representation from clinicians, clinical engineering, information technology, and risk management, to address alarm safety". — [Joint Commission SEA 50](https://digitalassets.jointcommission.org/api/public/content/f65e5c9df2b94000a99445e0a7877007)
- Drew et al. 2014 (PLoS ONE; 461 ICU patients, 77 beds, 31 days): 2,558,760 unique alarms; 381,560 (14.9%) audible, i.e. "187 per bed per day"; "88.8% of the 12,671 annotated arrhythmia alarms were false positives"; per-type false rates: ventricular bradycardia 96.7%, accelerated ventricular rhythm 94.8%, pause 87.7%, VT 86.8%, asystole 67.0%. — [Drew et al. 2014, PMC4206416](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4206416)
- Drew et al. design remedies: adaptive thresholds prompted by the device ("Mean HR = 130; do you want increase high HR setting?"), configurable delays, alerting only on onset/termination of a persistent condition (atrial fibrillation) rather than its continuation, and self-diagnosis of sensor quality (electrode impedance). — [Drew et al. 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4206416)

### Inferences
- The ISA-18.2 definition ("requires operator action… definable consequence… time to respond") is a crisp admission test for the dashboard's top tier: if no analyst action follows from a signal, it is information, not an alarm, and belongs in the browsable layer.
- The "5–10 minutes per alarm" response-time logic gives a sizing rule independent of paging: if an analyst needs ~10 min to assess a flagged user/day (consistent with Vectra's "10+ minutes per alert"), then 30 min/day of attention supports ~3 top-tier items per analyst per day; the daily list should be capped accordingly and overflow pushed to a lower tier.
- Alarm-flood handling (>10 in 10 min) maps to "incident days": when a daily run flags many users at once (e.g., a product launch or model change), the UI should collapse the flood into one explained event rather than list every user, and the detector should recognise fleet-wide shifts as a single cause.
- "Standing/stale alarms = 0" maps to: a flagged pattern that persists for days without being resolved or explained should either be acknowledged with a benign explanation (and suppressed) or escalated — it should not sit on the list indefinitely.
- "Bad actors <5%" maps to a tuning dashboard: track which detector/user pairs generate most flags; if the top 10 produce most of the volume, they are tuning targets, not findings.
- The 80/15/5 priority split and Drew's "alert on onset, not continuation" give concrete defaults for a daily system: most items should be low-tier context, and a persistent elevated state should generate one onset signal and one resolution signal, not a daily repeat.
- Joint Commission's cross-disciplinary review and "priorities drive technology" map to a periodic (e.g., monthly) review of detector set and thresholds with the people who act on the findings (HR/legal/IT), not only the dashboard builder.

### Gaps
- Primary EEMUA 191 / ISA-18.2 / IEC 62682 text is paywalled; all figures are from vendor summaries that claim to copy the standards' tables. Exact wording (e.g., "very likely to be acceptable", "manageable", "over-demanding" categories) is not verified.
- No source found that validates the 80/15/5 split empirically for non-industrial (security or analytics) alerting; it is a rationalisation target, not an empirical optimum.

## KQ5. Specific guidance on severity levels: how many, how to name them, what proportion each should have.

### Takeaway
Practitioner schemes use 3–5 levels with the top levels defined by *response expectation* (who acts, how fast) rather than by detector confidence; industrial standards recommend three annunciated priorities with a strongly skewed 80/15/5 distribution so the top level stays rare; Google effectively uses two human-facing channels (page, ticket) plus a non-notifying dashboard tier. "If in doubt, treat as the higher level" (PagerDuty) and "page only on novel, actionable" (Google) are the two tie-breakers.

### Cited Findings
- PagerDuty incident response guide, five levels defined by response: SEV-1 "Critical issue that warrants public notification and liaison with executive teams."; SEV-2 "Critical system issue actively impacting many customers' ability to use the product."; SEV-3 "Stability or minor customer-impacting issues that require immediate attention from service owners." (high-urgency page); SEV-4 "Minor issues requiring action, but not affecting customer ability to use the product." (low-urgency page, worked above routine tasks); SEV-5 "Cosmetic issues or bugs, not affecting customer ability to use the product." (ticket). "If you are unsure which level an incident is (e.g. not sure if SEV-2 or SEV-1), treat it as the higher one." — [PagerDuty Incident Response: Severity Levels](https://response.pagerduty.com/before/severity_levels/) **[vendor, but widely reused open guide]**
- Google: two human-action channels — "pages and tickets are the only valid ways to get a human to take action" — plus dashboards; "important but subcritical alerts to their ticket queues. All other alerts should be retained as informational data for status dashboards." — [SRE Workbook](https://sre.google/workbook/alerting-on-slos/); [SRE Book ch.10](https://sre.google/sre-book/practical-alerting/)
- Google's burn-rate tiers tie severity to *rate of damage* and *time available*: 2% budget in 1h → page; 5% in 6h → page; 10% in 3 days → ticket. — [SRE Workbook](https://sre.google/workbook/alerting-on-slos/)
- ISA-18.2/EEMUA 191: three annunciated priorities, target distribution 5% high / 15% medium / 80% low; un-rationalised plants show ~28/41/31 — i.e., failure mode is too many high/medium alarms. — [iFactory](https://ifactoryapp.com/blog/alarm-management-rationalization-chemical) **[vendor]**
- PagerDuty: "While all alerts are important, some may not be urgent." Low-urgency items get a separate path ("Save non-severe incidents for morning"). — [PagerDuty](https://www.pagerduty.com/blog/uncategorized/lets-talk-about-alert-fatigue-2/) **[vendor]**
- Joint Commission links harm to alarms "not customized to the individual patient or patient population" — i.e., a severity assigned by a global default rather than by the subject's own baseline. — [Joint Commission SEA 50](https://digitalassets.jointcommission.org/api/public/content/f65e5c9df2b94000a99445e0a7877007)

### Inferences
- For a daily-batch dashboard, three tiers are sufficient and match both the Google (page/ticket/dashboard) and ISA (high/medium/low) structures: (1) "Review today" — rare, novel, action-defined, expected ≤~5% of flagged items; (2) "Review this week" — accumulating or moderate deviations (~15%); (3) "Context / watch" — not notified, visible on drill-down (~80%). Naming by *expected analyst action and deadline* ("Review today", "Review this week", "Watch") avoids both confidence jargon and accusatory labels.
- Severity should be a function of consequence × time-to-act × novelty (the ISA definition and Google's burn-rate logic), not of statistical z-score alone; a huge deviation that recurs daily and has been explained is low severity, while a modest but first-ever pattern on a sensitive account may be high.
- PagerDuty's "when unsure, go higher" should be applied only at the boundary between tiers 1 and 2; applying it broadly would inflate tier 1 and recreate the 28/41/31 failure mode.
- Proportion targets should be monitored (share of items per tier per week) and used as a tuning signal exactly like the ISA priority-distribution KPI.

### Gaps
- No source gives an empirically validated number of levels for analyst review queues; 3-level (ISA), 2+dashboard (Google) and 5-level (PagerDuty) are all conventions.
- No source found that specifies naming conventions for non-security, non-ops contexts (e.g., HR-adjacent usage monitoring).

## KQ6. (Cross-cutting, from the objective) How to avoid "alert = guilt" framing

### Takeaway
The strongest evidence is Alahmadi et al.'s benign-trigger finding: most alarms that match correctly are explained by legitimate activity, so an alarm should be framed as "pattern matched; needs an explanation" rather than "wrongdoing detected". Insider-risk guidance echoes this: programmes should "identify risk, vulnerability, and anomalous activity, not … create an organization in which every employee is treated as a potential offender", and the UI/process language should reflect that. (Note: the direct UX literature on wording of anomaly alerts is thin; this section is partly inferential.)

### Cited Findings
- "benign triggers are true alarms; meaning they match an existing signature (e.g., vulnerable java version) but the organization chooses to ignore it (e.g., due to legacy systems)." and the recommendation that tools "use false alarms or benign triggers as an alternative metric to FPs, which is general and vague." — [Alahmadi et al. 2022](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/sec22-alahmadi.pdf)
- Analysts' own triage starts from benign hypotheses (acquisition explains outbound traffic; Dubai working week explains no traffic on Friday). — [Alahmadi et al. 2022 §7.3](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/sec22-alahmadi.pdf)
- Insider-risk programme guidance (industry): "A strong Insider Threat Management Program should be designed to identify risk, vulnerability, and anomalous activity, not to create an organization in which every employee is treated as a potential offender. If insider threat programs begin with the assumption that employees themselves are the problem, the natural response is greater surveillance." — [Crisis24, "Balancing trust, privacy and monitoring in insider risk programs"](https://www.crisis24.com/articles/balancing-trust-privacy-and-monitoring-in-insider-risk-programs) **[vendor]**
- CERT trust-trap pattern: organisations fall into a cycle where "they do not detect suspicious insider behavior, so they trust their insiders and do not monitor them, consequently not detecting suspicious insider behavior" — monitoring itself is defended as a trust-maintaining control if fair and communicated. — [Mundie et al., "A pattern for trust trap mitigation", PLoP 2011](https://Hillside.net/plop/2011/papers/proceedings/papers/mundie.pdf)
- Some UEBA designs keep identities pseudonymous "until suspicious activity is detected, which helps alleviate privacy concerns and removes bias and false positives from security teams" — [search summary of vendor material](https://www.exabeam.com/wp-content/uploads/Lin-Insider-Threat-Detection.pdf) **[vendor]**
- Google's page criteria are framed around the *system* and the *user impact*, never the operator; and the SRE book treats "rote, algorithmic responses" as a design smell, pushing responsibility onto the alert designer rather than the responder. — [SRE Book ch.6](https://sre.google/sre-book/monitoring-distributed-systems/)

### Inferences
- Label vocabulary: use "signal", "deviation", "needs explanation", "pattern" rather than "violation", "abuse", "suspect"; present the detector's comparison ("3.4× the user's own 28-day median on the same day kind") rather than a verdict.
- Disposition vocabulary should offer at least three outcomes — *explained (benign)*, *tuning issue (detector/baseline)*, *escalate* — so that the default close action is not "false positive" (which stigmatises the detector) or "confirmed" (which stigmatises the person).
- Store benign explanations as first-class data and use them to suppress repeats (Alahmadi's Reliable/Contextual requirements), which both reduces load and prevents a user from being re-flagged for a known, approved reason.
- Consider pseudonymous display at the list level with identity revealed on drill-down, as in the UEBA pattern, to reduce bias during initial triage by a small team that knows the individuals.
- Because the audience is 1–3 analysts who likely know the monitored users personally, the Joint Commission's cross-disciplinary review principle suggests a defined escalation path (who outside the analysts decides on consequences) so that the dashboard itself never functions as a judgment.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed UX study was found that tests alert wording (e.g., "suspicious" vs "unusual") on analyst behaviour or on monitored-employee trust; the vocabulary recommendations above are inferences from the benign-trigger finding and insider-risk programme guidance, not measured results.
- CERT/CISA primary documents on insider-risk programme fairness were not fetched directly; the quotes are from secondary industry summaries.
